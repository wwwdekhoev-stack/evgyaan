Beyond the vehicles and the sales numbers, a set of policy and industry-structure stories from the past few days shows where India's EV ecosystem still has work to do.

## India's EV policy is shifting focus

A report on 27 October said India's **next phase of EV policy** is moving away from pure subsidies, toward **rare-earth materials, cybersecurity, battery manufacturing, and attracting foreign plants**. This is a meaningful shift in emphasis — subsidies got EVs off the ground in India, but a longer-term strategy has to deal with the supply-chain vulnerabilities that subsidies alone don't fix.

## State-level tax uncertainty is a real industry headache

At the **EV Conclave on 28 October**, industry leaders flagged **uncertainty around state-level taxes** and the resulting **price parity issues** as a major concern. Different states taxing EVs differently makes it genuinely hard for manufacturers to price consistently nationwide, and it's exactly the kind of structural friction that can quietly slow down adoption even while demand itself is strong.

## India doesn't have enough trained EV technicians

Also flagged on 28 October: the **ASDC** (Automotive Skills Development Council) said countries like **Germany and Australia are actively seeking Indian EV technicians** — while India itself faces a **domestic skills shortage**. That's a slightly uncomfortable position to be in: trained enough to be wanted abroad, but not present in enough numbers to fully service the EVs already on Indian roads. As EV sales keep climbing, after-sales service capacity is going to matter as much as factory capacity.

## A new way to finance electric bus fleets

Finally, also at the EV Conclave, it was reported that operators are increasingly **securitising municipal contracts** — effectively using the guaranteed, long-term revenue from government bus contracts as collateral — to raise financing for electric bus fleets. This is a genuinely useful innovation: electric buses are expensive upfront, and a financing structure built around a government contract's reliability could make it easier for more cities to go electric without needing the operator to have deep pockets of their own.

## The bigger picture

None of these four stories will make headlines the way a new scooter launch does. But policy direction, tax clarity, skilled manpower and financing structures are exactly the kind of unglamorous groundwork that decides whether India's EV growth of the last few years turns into a durable, long-term shift — or hits a ceiling because the supporting ecosystem didn't keep pace.
