Ninety days, 7,571 km. That is 84 km a day, every day, on an electric motorcycle that has been on sale in India for barely six months. The rider doing it came off a Hero Splendor, and he has not bought petrol for his daily running since.

We sat down with him for an ownership review of the Oben Rorr EVO. Three months of hard, unglamorous Delhi commuting is a real test, and his verdict at the end of it was 9 out of 10. Here is what earned that score.

## A ₹5,000-6,000 petrol bill, down to about ₹600

On the Splendor he was spending ₹5,000 to ₹6,000 a month on fuel. At his running of roughly 2,520 km a month, that is about ₹2 per km.

Now run the same distance on the Rorr EVO. The battery is 3.4 kWh. A full charge from near-empty pulls roughly 3.4 units plus charging losses, so call it ₹30 at a ₹8 per unit domestic tariff. His best single-charge run is 135 km. That works out to about 22 paise per km.

Over 7,571 km that comes to somewhere around 210 units of electricity. Under ₹2,000 in three months, against ₹15,000 to ₹18,000 of petrol for the same distance. When we asked if the money was actually being saved, his answer was flat: all of it is.

And he has not even claimed his subsidy yet. The dealer told him ₹5,000 to ₹7,000 was available. Under PM E-DRIVE the incentive for electric two-wheelers is ₹2,500 per kWh capped at ₹5,000 per vehicle, for bikes registered up to 31 March 2028. On a 3.4 kWh pack the per-kWh maths comes to ₹8,500, so the cap applies and he should expect ₹5,000. Claims close on 31 December 2027, so that money is still sitting there for him.

## 135 km on a charge, and no range anxiety

Oben rates the Rorr EVO at 180 km IDC. The bike has four riding modes, Smart IQ, Eco, City and Havoc, and he runs it almost entirely in Smart IQ, where the display shows up to about 140 km.

His longest single-charge run is 135 km. For a motorcycle being ridden at real commuter speeds in Delhi traffic, 75% of the certified figure is a solid return, and it comfortably covers his 90-100 km day with 20-25% still left in the pack when he gets home. Charging takes three to four hours on the standard charger, which for someone plugging in overnight is a non-issue.

He has also seen the claimed 110 km/h top speed himself, with no vibration and nothing unsettling at that speed. Havoc is the fast mode and it does what fast modes do: plenty of speed, less range. Smart IQ is where he lives.

Range anxiety has not happened once, and his reason is the right one for most buyers. He knows exactly how far he rides in a day, he plans around it, and he keeps a petrol bike for the rare unplanned long run. Plan your day and the bike simply does it.

## Built like a real motorcycle

A lot of first-time EV buyers worry the bike will feel like a toy. This one does not. At 140 kg it has presence, and he described sitting on it the way he would a Pulsar or an Apache. The seat holds up over 100 km a day without leaving him tired, which matters more than any spec on the sheet.

The practical bits have surprised him more than the performance. There is 4 litres of storage in the tank pod and 6 litres under the seat, enough for a sweatshirt when the evening turns cold, a wallet, or anything he does not want in his pockets. His favourite feature turned out to be the USB port. His phone died once when he had a payment to make, he plugged in, charged up and sorted it out without having to find anyone else's charger. That was the moment, he said, that he felt he had bought something good.

## Two things Oben should add

The score is 9 and not 10 for one reason: chain noise.

The Rorr EVO is chain-driven and the chain is louder than it should be. He raised it with the company and was told it would settle as the bike ran in. At 7,571 km it is still there. On a bike whose biggest everyday pleasure is how quiet it is, that is the one note out of tune, and it is a fixable one.

The second is a centre stand, which the bike does not get. On a chain-drive motorcycle that matters, because greasing the chain, cleaning it and washing the bike properly all need the rear wheel to spin freely. He also added crash guards on both sides for ₹2,000 at Karol Bagh, plus ₹900 on another accessory and ₹1,200 on mirrors, around ₹4,100 of fitment. A factory crash guard, a centre stand and a saree guard would close almost every gap he named, and none of them is hard for Oben to do.

## Service: parts free, process still maturing

His lock failed and Oben replaced it free of cost under warranty, done on 29 October. The warranty did its job and he paid nothing.

What took time was the part. It had to be ordered in, an internal approval had to clear, and start to finish it was about a month. That is a young brand's supply chain catching up with a bike that is selling, and it is the kind of thing that improves as volumes grow. On everything else, he said, the dealer sorted the small issues on the spot.

The first service was the 1,000 km one. It cost ₹990 and was a routine check of brakes and fasteners. No surprise bills in three months.

## What this means for you

If your riding is predictable and heavy, the Rorr EVO makes a strong case. The 3.4 kWh LFP pack carries an 8-year or 80,000 km warranty and the bike 3 years or 40,000 km, and this owner has already put nearly a fifth of that bike warranty on the odometer in 90 days with no mechanical failure, no heating complaints and no breakdown.

What you get is a 140 kg motorcycle that rides like a 150cc commuter, does a genuine 135 km on a charge, carries 10 litres of luggage between two compartments, costs roughly a tenth of what petrol was costing him, and still has a ₹5,000 subsidy waiting to be claimed.

What you should budget for is a crash guard and a centre stand, and accept that an uncommon spare can take a few weeks. For him, none of that came close to outweighing the rest. People told him electric was still two or three years away from being properly usable. Three months and 7,571 km later, he has not felt it.
